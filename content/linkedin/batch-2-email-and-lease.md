## Post 1: The Break-Up Email (Blog 3)

The email that gets the most responses in my prospecting sequence is the one where I tell them I'm going away.

It's called the break-up email.

Subject line: "Should I close the file?"

Body is simple. Something like:

"I've reached out a few times and haven't heard back. Totally understand — timing might not be right. I'm going to close out your file on my end, but if anything changes with your space needs, I'm here."

That's it. No pitch. No pressure.

And it consistently outperforms every other email in the sequence.

Why?

Loss aversion.

People don't like losing access to something — even something they never asked for. The moment you signal you're walking away, the dynamic shifts. Suddenly they're the ones who might miss out.

I've seen this email pull replies from prospects who ghosted 3 previous touches.

The key is you have to actually mean it. If they don't respond, you move on. That's what makes it work — it's not a trick, it's a real boundary.

## Post 2: The Template Teaser — Lease Expiration Trigger (Blog 3)

Here's the exact cold email I send when I find a tenant whose lease expires in 12-18 months.

Subject: [Submarket] office market — shift worth knowing about

"Hi [Name],

I've been tracking the [submarket] market closely and wanted to flag something. Vacancy in your area has moved to [X]%, which is creating some leverage for tenants that wasn't there six months ago.

Given your company's footprint at [address], there may be an opportunity to renegotiate terms or explore options that better fit where you're headed.

I put together a short market brief on what's happening in [submarket]. Happy to share it if useful — no strings attached.

Best,
[Your name]"

Notice what's NOT in this email:

→ No "I know your lease is expiring"
→ No pitch for a meeting
→ No talking about yourself
→ No attachments

It leads with value. It positions you as someone who understands their market. And it gives them a low-friction way to engage.

## Post 3: The Deliverability Warning (Blog 3)

Your cold emails might be landing in spam and you don't even know it.

I talk to brokers all the time who say "cold email doesn't work." Then I look at their setup and the problem is obvious.

They're sending from their primary domain. No warm-up. No authentication. And they're blasting 200 emails on day one.

Here's what's actually happening: Gmail and Outlook are quietly routing those emails straight to junk. No bounce notification. No error message. Just silence.

A few things that fix this:

→ Send from a secondary domain (not your main brokerage domain)
→ Set up SPF, DKIM, and DMARC authentication — your IT person or email provider can do this in 30 minutes
→ Warm up the domain for 2-3 weeks before sending prospecting emails
→ Start with 10-15 sends per day and scale slowly
→ Clean your list — remove bounces and invalid addresses immediately

The benchmarks that matter in CRE cold email:

→ 40-60% open rate is good
→ 5-15% reply rate is solid
→ Anything below 30% open rate means you have a deliverability problem, not a messaging problem

Fix the infrastructure first. Then worry about the copy.

## Post 4: The Timing Post (Blog 4)

If you're reaching out to tenants 6 months before their lease expires, you're already too late.

Here's what's actually happening at 6 months out:

→ The landlord has already started retention conversations
→ They've probably offered a renewal with a modest discount
→ The tenant is thinking "good enough" and losing motivation to explore the market
→ You're showing up as an interruption, not an advisor

The sweet spot is 12-18 months before expiration.

At 18 months, the tenant hasn't heard from their landlord yet. They're not in decision mode — they're in planning mode. That's when a broker adds the most value.

You get to be the one who says: "Here's what the market looks like. Here's what your options are. Let's build a strategy so when it's time to negotiate, you have leverage."

By the time the landlord comes knocking, your tenant already knows what the market will bear. That changes the entire negotiation.

The brokers who consistently win tenant rep deals aren't better closers.

They just start earlier than everyone else.

## Post 5: Free Data Sources for Lease Expirations (Blog 4)

You don't need CoStar to find lease expirations.

Don't get me wrong — CoStar is great if you have it. But it's not the only way to build a prospecting pipeline.

Here's where I find lease expiration data without expensive subscriptions:

→ SEC filings (10-K and 10-Q reports) — Public companies are required to disclose lease obligations. It's free on sec.gov and it's incredibly detailed. Square footage, expiration dates, locations, everything.

→ County property records — Many counties publish lease memorandums. These are public documents that include lease terms and dates. Check your county assessor or recorder's office.

→ News monitoring — Set Google Alerts for "[your market] + office lease" or "[company name] + headquarters." Companies announce relocations, expansions, and consolidations all the time.

→ Direct observation — Drive your market. For-lease signs, buildout activity, moving trucks. Sounds old school. Still works.

→ Your network — Other brokers, property managers, attorneys, contractors. The people who touch deals before you do. Buy them coffee.

The information is out there. Most brokers just default to one paid source and call it a day.

The ones who layer multiple sources build a pipeline their competitors can't replicate.

## Post 6: The "Don't Say This" Post (Blog 4)

Never open a cold email with "I noticed your lease is expiring."

I know — it seems like the perfect opener. It's specific. It's relevant. It shows you did your research.

But here's how the tenant reads it:

"How does this person know that? That's my private business information. This feels like surveillance."

You just triggered a defensive reaction in the first sentence. Everything after that gets filtered through suspicion instead of curiosity.

The fix is simple. Lead with market insight, not personal data.

Instead of:
"I noticed your lease at 500 Main St expires in March 2027..."

Try:
"I've been tracking the downtown submarket and there's a shift happening that could impact tenants in your building..."

Same prospect. Same intent. Completely different emotional response.

The first one says: "I'm watching you."
The second one says: "I know something valuable."

One makes them uncomfortable. The other makes them curious.

The best tenant rep prospecting doesn't feel like prospecting. It feels like a market expert sharing something worth knowing.

That positioning is what separates the brokers who get replies from the ones who get ignored.
