# The Tenant Rep Broker's Prospecting Playbook: A Complete Guide

Most tenant rep brokers know they should be prospecting more. Very few actually do it consistently. The ones who do tend to be the top producers in their market.

This isn't an accident. Prospecting is the single highest-leverage activity in tenant representation. One good conversation with the right decision-maker at the right time can produce a six-figure commission. But the gap between knowing that and actually filling your calendar with those conversations is where most brokers stall out.

This guide is designed to close that gap. It covers everything — from building your target list to crafting messages that get responses to systematizing follow-up so nothing slips through the cracks. Whether you're a first-year associate or a 20-year veteran looking to sharpen your process, this is the playbook.

Print it out. Bookmark it. Use it tomorrow morning.

---

## 1. Why Prospecting Is Different for Tenant Reps

If you came up on the landlord side or in investment sales, you need to rewire how you think about prospecting. The mechanics are fundamentally different.

**Landlord reps prospect properties.** They compete for listings by pitching owners on their marketing plan, their track record, their team. The universe of prospects is relatively defined — you can see every building in your submarket. The pitch is: "Let me lease your building."

**Investment sales brokers prospect deals.** They look for owners who might sell, track hold periods, watch for distress signals. Again, the universe is finite and largely public. The pitch is: "I can get you the best price."

**Tenant reps prospect companies.** This is a different animal entirely. You're not looking for a building or a deal — you're looking for a business that will need space at some point in the future. Your universe of prospects is every company in your market above a certain size. The pitch is: "I can save you money and time on your next lease."

This distinction matters because it changes everything about your approach:

- **Timing is harder to pin down.** A company's lease expiration isn't public the way a building's vacancy is. You have to dig for it.
- **Decision-makers are harder to identify.** It might be the CEO, the CFO, the VP of Operations, the Head of Real Estate, or an office manager. It varies by company size and industry.
- **The need isn't always obvious.** A landlord with 40% vacancy knows they need help. A company with 18 months left on their lease might not be thinking about it yet — which is exactly when you want to reach them.
- **You're selling an advisory relationship, not a transaction.** Tenant rep is a consultative sale. You need to build trust before you earn the assignment.

The brokers who understand these differences build their prospecting systems around them. The brokers who don't end up blasting generic emails to purchased lists and wondering why nobody responds.

## 2. Building Your Target List

Your target list is the foundation of everything. A mediocre message sent to the right person at the right time will outperform a perfect message sent to the wrong list every single day.

### Where to Find Prospects

**Lease expiration databases.** This is the most direct signal you can find. If a company's lease expires in 12-24 months, they are going to make a decision about their space — whether they know it yet or not. Sources include CoStar, CompStak, and local county records. Some brokers also build their own databases by tracking lease comps over time and estimating expirations.

**CoStar and other CRE platforms.** Beyond lease expirations, CoStar's tenant data can show you which companies occupy which buildings, their approximate square footage, and sometimes their lease terms. Cross-reference this with building-level data to identify tenants in buildings with known issues — deferred maintenance, ownership changes, upcoming renovations that might cause disruption.

**LinkedIn.** Search for companies in your market by industry, headcount, and growth stage. LinkedIn Sales Navigator lets you filter by company size, headquarters location, and recent headcount changes. A company that grew from 50 to 120 employees in the past year almost certainly needs more space.

**Local business journals.** Publications like your city's Business Journal, Crain's, or similar outlets are goldmines. They report on company expansions, relocations, new market entries, and funding rounds — all of which signal potential space needs. Set up daily alerts.

**SEC filings (for public companies).** 10-K filings contain a section on properties that lists every lease a public company holds, including locations, square footage, and expiration dates. This is free, public information. If you focus on mid-cap or large-cap companies with space in your market, this is one of the most underused data sources in tenant rep.

**Google Alerts.** Set up alerts for phrases like "[your city] office expansion," "[your city] company relocation," "hiring [your city]," and the names of specific companies you're tracking. You want to know when a prospect makes news before your competitors do.

**Venture capital and private equity announcements.** A company that just raised a Series B is about to hire aggressively. A company that just got acquired by a PE firm is about to consolidate or restructure their real estate. Track funding announcements through Crunchbase, PitchBook, or even Twitter.

**Your own network.** Every broker you talk to, every attorney, every contractor, every architect knows companies that are thinking about their space. Most brokers are terrible at systematically mining their network for referrals. Schedule quarterly check-ins with your top 20 referral sources and ask one simple question: "Who do you know that's thinking about their space?"

### How to Prioritize Your List

Not every prospect deserves the same effort. Prioritize based on three factors:

1. **Timing.** Companies with lease expirations in the next 6-24 months are highest priority. Under 6 months, they've likely already engaged a broker or decided to renew. Over 24 months, they're unlikely to act.

2. **Size.** Bigger deals justify more effort. A company leasing 50,000 SF is worth more outreach than one leasing 2,000 SF. Know your minimum deal size and be honest about it.

3. **Likelihood of engagement.** Companies showing growth signals (hiring, funding, expansion news) are more likely to respond than companies in maintenance mode. Companies in buildings with known problems (ownership disputes, deferred maintenance, upcoming lease-up pressure) are more likely to be thinking about alternatives.

A practical approach: tier your list into A, B, and C prospects. Your A-list (high timing + high size + strong signals) gets personalized, multi-touch outreach. Your B-list gets semi-personalized outreach. Your C-list gets scaled outreach with light personalization. More on this in the outreach section.

## 3. Research Before Outreach

The single biggest differentiator between outreach that gets responses and outreach that gets deleted is whether it's obvious you did your homework.

This doesn't mean spending an hour on every prospect. It means spending five minutes gathering enough information to write a message that couldn't have been sent to anyone else.

### The 5-Minute Research Framework

For each prospect, answer these five questions before you reach out:

**1. What does the company do, and how big are they?** (30 seconds) Check their website and LinkedIn. Know their industry, approximate headcount, and whether they're growing or stable. You should never send an email that makes it clear you don't know what the company does.

**2. Where are they located, and what's their current space situation?** (60 seconds) Check CoStar or your CRE database. What building are they in? How much space do they have? When does their lease expire, if you can find it? Is the building well-regarded or problematic?

**3. Are there any growth or change signals?** (60 seconds) Check recent news, LinkedIn headcount trends, and job postings. A company posting 15 open roles on their careers page is a company that's going to need more space. A company that just announced a new product line might need lab or warehouse space.

**4. What's the relevant market context?** (60 seconds) What's happening in their submarket? Are rents rising? Is there new supply coming online? Are comparable tenants getting aggressive concession packages? You want at least one market insight you can lead with.

**5. Who is the right person to contact?** (60 seconds) For companies under 50 employees, it's usually the CEO or COO. For 50-200 employees, look for a VP of Operations, Head of People, or CFO. For 200+, there may be a dedicated real estate or facilities person. Check LinkedIn. If you can't find the right person, the CEO's executive assistant is often the best starting point.

Total time: five minutes. That five minutes is the difference between "Dear Business Owner, I'd love to help with your real estate needs" and "Hi Sarah — I noticed Acme is hiring 12 engineers in Austin this quarter. With Eastside submarket vacancy at 8% and tightening, it might be worth exploring your options before Q4. Happy to share what we're seeing."

The first message gets deleted. The second gets a reply.

## 4. Multi-Channel Outreach Cadence

One email does not constitute outreach. Neither do two. The data on this is unambiguous: most positive responses come after the third, fourth, or fifth touch. And mixing channels — email, phone, LinkedIn — dramatically increases your odds of getting through.

Here is a proven 21-day outreach cadence for tenant rep prospecting:

### The 21-Day Cadence

| Day | Channel | Action |
|-----|---------|--------|
| 1 | LinkedIn | Send a connection request with a short, personalized note. No pitch. |
| 3 | Email | Send Email 1 — lead with a market insight relevant to their situation. |
| 5 | Phone | Call. If you get voicemail, leave a brief message referencing your email. |
| 8 | Email | Send Email 2 — share a specific data point or case study. |
| 10 | LinkedIn | Send a LinkedIn message (if connected) or comment on their recent post. |
| 14 | Email | Send Email 3 — shorter, more direct. "Is this on your radar?" |
| 17 | Phone | Second call attempt. |
| 21 | Email | Break-up email. "I'll assume the timing isn't right. I'll check back in 6 months." |

### Why This Works

**LinkedIn first** establishes familiarity. When your email arrives two days later, your name isn't completely cold — they've seen your face and title in their connection requests.

**Email carries the substance.** This is where you demonstrate expertise and give them a reason to engage. Each email should offer something slightly different — a market insight, a relevant comp, a case study.

**Phone adds urgency and humanity.** Email is easy to ignore. A voicemail from someone who clearly knows your situation is harder to dismiss. You don't need to be a cold-calling machine — two well-timed calls in 21 days is enough.

**The break-up email is counterintuitively effective.** By signaling that you're going to stop reaching out, you remove pressure. Many brokers report that their break-up email gets more responses than any other message in the sequence.

### Adjusting by Prospect Tier

- **A-tier prospects:** Run the full 21-day cadence with fully personalized messages. Consider adding a handwritten note or a relevant market report as an additional touch.
- **B-tier prospects:** Run the full cadence with semi-personalized messages (templated structure, customized opening line and key detail).
- **C-tier prospects:** Use a simplified cadence — three emails over 14 days with light personalization.

## 5. Crafting Your Message

The messages themselves matter. A lot. Here's what separates effective CRE outreach from the noise.

### Lead with Market Insight, Not Your Resume

The most common mistake in broker outreach is leading with yourself. "I'm a senior broker at XYZ with 15 years of experience and $500M in transactions." Nobody cares. Not in the first message.

What they care about is: **"Do you know something I don't?"**

Lead with a market insight, a data point, or an observation about their specific situation. Examples:

- "Sublease availability in Midtown has doubled since Q3 — there are options on the table right now that won't exist in six months."
- "I noticed your lease at 200 Main is up next fall. Three other tenants in that building are already exploring relocations, which could give you negotiating leverage if you decide to stay."
- "Companies your size in biotech are locking in 7-year terms at $45 PSF with 18 months of free rent. That's a market we haven't seen in five years."

Each of these messages does the same thing: it demonstrates that you understand their world and that talking to you would be worth 15 minutes of their time.

### Show Your Homework

Reference something specific — their recent funding round, a job posting, a news article, their current building. One concrete detail signals that this isn't a mass email. It takes 30 seconds to add and doubles your response rate.

### Keep It Short

Your first email should be 4-6 sentences. That's it. You're not trying to close a deal — you're trying to start a conversation. Every sentence should earn the next one.

Here's a structure that works:

1. **Hook** — one sentence referencing their situation or a market insight (1 sentence)
2. **Context** — why this is relevant to them specifically (1-2 sentences)
3. **Credibility** — a brief, relevant proof point (1 sentence)
4. **Ask** — a low-commitment CTA (1 sentence)

**Example:**

> Subject: Office market shift in South Lake Union
>
> Hi David,
>
> Sublease inventory in SLU jumped 30% last quarter — landlords are starting to offer concession packages we haven't seen since 2020. With your lease at Westpark coming up next year, the timing to test the market could be strong.
>
> We just helped a 40-person SaaS company in your building negotiate a relocation that cut their occupancy costs by 22%.
>
> Worth a 15-minute call to walk through what we're seeing?

That's 78 words. It has a market insight, a specific reference to their situation, a relevant proof point, and a soft ask. It took five minutes to research and two minutes to write. And it will outperform a 400-word email about your credentials every time.

### What to Say on the Phone

Keep voicemails under 30 seconds. State your name, firm, one reason you're calling ("I sent you some data on the SLU submarket that's relevant to your lease at Westpark"), and your number. That's it. Don't try to pitch on a voicemail.

If you get the prospect live, your goal is the same as email: start a conversation, not close a deal. Ask a question: "How are you thinking about your space as you head into next year?" Listen more than you talk.

## 6. Follow-Up Systems

Here's the uncomfortable truth: most brokers don't fail at prospecting because they can't write a good email or make a good call. They fail because they don't follow up.

The math on this is stark. If you reach out to 50 prospects and get 5 responses, what happens to the other 45? If the answer is "nothing" — and for most brokers it is — you're leaving the majority of your potential pipeline on the table.

### Why Follow-Up Breaks Down

Three reasons:

1. **It's not urgent.** There's always a live deal, a client request, or a proposal to write. Follow-up on a cold prospect feels like it can wait. So it waits. And waits.

2. **There's no system.** Follow-up lives in the broker's head, in scattered notes, or in a CRM that nobody actually uses. When there's no system, follow-up depends entirely on memory and willpower — both of which are unreliable.

3. **It feels pointless.** After three unanswered emails, most brokers convince themselves the prospect isn't interested. But "not interested right now" is not the same as "not interested ever." The company that ignores you in March might have a lease event in September that makes your outreach suddenly relevant.

### Building a Follow-Up System That Works

**Use a CRM — even a simple one.** You don't need Salesforce. You need a system that tells you, every morning, who you need to follow up with today. HubSpot's free tier, Pipedrive, or even a well-structured spreadsheet can work. The key is: every prospect goes into the system, every interaction gets logged, and every next step gets a date.

**Set follow-up cadences for every stage.** Active outreach (the 21-day cadence) is just the beginning. After the initial cadence, move non-responders to a long-term nurture track: one touch per quarter with a relevant market update or insight. When their lease event gets closer, move them back to active outreach.

**Block time for follow-up.** Thirty minutes every morning, before you open your inbox. Pull up your CRM, see who's due for a touch, and execute. This is non-negotiable prospecting time. Protect it like you'd protect a client meeting.

**Track your pipeline stages.** At minimum, you need: New Prospect → Active Outreach → Responded → Meeting Scheduled → Proposal/Pitch → Engaged → Closed. Know how many prospects you have at each stage at all times.

## 7. Tracking and Measuring

You can't improve what you don't measure. And most brokers don't measure their prospecting at all — they just have a vague sense of whether they're "doing enough."

### The Metrics That Matter

**Outreach volume.** How many new prospects are you touching per week? This is the top of your funnel. If you're not reaching enough people, nothing else matters.

- **Benchmark:** Top-producing tenant reps typically make 30-50 new prospect touches per week (across all channels). This doesn't mean 50 cold calls — it means 50 total touches including emails, calls, and LinkedIn messages.

**Response rate.** What percentage of prospects respond to your outreach (positively or negatively)? This tells you whether your messaging is working.

- **Benchmark:** A 10-15% response rate on cold email outreach is strong in CRE. Below 5% means your messaging, your list, or both need work.

**Meeting conversion rate.** What percentage of responses convert to an actual meeting or call? This tells you whether you're qualifying well and making a compelling case for the meeting.

- **Benchmark:** 40-60% of positive responses should convert to meetings. If prospects are responding but not booking, your follow-up or scheduling process needs attention.

**Meetings booked per week.** This is the most important leading indicator of future revenue. Everything upstream feeds into this number.

- **Benchmark:** 2-4 new prospect meetings per week is a strong pace for a tenant rep broker focused on mid-to-large transactions.

**Pipeline generated.** What's the total potential commission value of the prospects in your active pipeline? This tells you whether you're on track to hit your annual revenue target.

- **Benchmark:** You should have 3-5x your annual revenue target in total pipeline at any given time, because not every deal will close and not every prospect will engage.

### How to Track

A simple weekly scorecard works. Every Friday, record:

- New prospects contacted this week
- Responses received
- Meetings booked
- Pipeline value added
- Deals advanced to next stage

Review the trend monthly. If outreach volume is up but response rate is down, it's a messaging problem. If response rate is up but meetings are flat, it's a conversion problem. The data tells you exactly where to focus.

## 8. Common Mistakes

After working with hundreds of tenant rep brokers, these are the five prospecting mistakes we see most often.

### Mistake 1: Spraying and Praying

Sending 500 generic emails to a purchased list and hoping for the best. This approach has a response rate near zero, damages your reputation, and trains prospects to ignore you. Fifty well-researched, personalized outreach attempts will outperform 500 generic ones every time.

### Mistake 2: Leading with Your Credentials

"I'm a top broker with $2B in transactions." Great. So is every other broker sending cold emails. Prospects don't care about your resume — they care about their problem. Lead with what you know about their situation, not what you've accomplished.

### Mistake 3: Giving Up Too Early

The average broker sends one or two emails, gets no response, and moves on. The data shows that most responses come after 4-7 touches. If you're stopping after two, you're quitting right before the payoff. Run the full cadence.

### Mistake 4: Not Tracking Anything

If you can't tell me how many prospects you contacted last week, what your response rate was, or how many meetings you booked, you're flying blind. Prospecting without metrics is like managing a building without financials — you're guessing, and guessing doesn't scale.

### Mistake 5: Treating Prospecting as Optional

Prospecting only when your pipeline is dry creates a feast-or-famine cycle. You close a deal, get busy on execution, stop prospecting, finish the deal, realize your pipeline is empty, panic, start prospecting again. The best brokers prospect consistently regardless of how busy they are. Thirty minutes a day, every day.

## 9. Scaling Your Outreach

Everything in this playbook works. It also takes time — time that most brokers don't have enough of, especially as their deal load grows.

This is the core tension in tenant representation: the activity that generates future revenue (prospecting) competes directly with the activity that generates current revenue (working live deals). As you get busier, prospecting is the first thing to drop. And then your pipeline dries up six months later.

### Where AI and Automation Fit

The most time-consuming parts of prospecting — building lists, researching prospects, personalizing messages, scheduling follow-ups — are exactly the tasks where AI and automation can help without sacrificing quality.

Consider what a typical prospecting workflow looks like today:

- 30 minutes building and updating your target list
- 20 minutes researching a batch of 5 prospects
- 25 minutes writing personalized emails
- 15 minutes logging everything in your CRM
- 10 minutes scheduling follow-up tasks

That's over 90 minutes to contact 5 prospects. At that pace, reaching 50 prospects per week requires 15+ hours of pure prospecting time. Most brokers simply can't sustain that while also managing active deals, client relationships, and the rest of their business.

With the right tools, much of the research, drafting, and CRM management can be automated or accelerated — letting you maintain a high-volume, high-quality outreach program in a fraction of the time. Brokers using AI-assisted prospecting tools routinely 3-5x their outreach volume while keeping the personalization that makes their messages effective.

The key is that automation should amplify your expertise, not replace it. The market insight, the strategic thinking, the relationship — that's you. The data gathering, the first-draft writing, the CRM logging — that's where technology earns its keep.

## Putting It All Together

Prospecting in tenant representation isn't complicated. It's demanding. The brokers who build a system, execute consistently, and refine based on data will always outperform those who rely on referrals and hope.

Here's the short version of the entire playbook:

1. **Build a prioritized target list** using lease expirations, growth signals, and market data.
2. **Spend five minutes researching each prospect** before you reach out.
3. **Run a multi-channel, 21-day outreach cadence** that mixes email, phone, and LinkedIn.
4. **Lead every message with market insight**, not your resume.
5. **Systematize your follow-up** so no prospect falls through the cracks.
6. **Track your metrics weekly** and adjust based on what the data tells you.
7. **Avoid the five common mistakes** that kill most brokers' prospecting efforts.
8. **Use technology to scale** when manual effort hits its ceiling.

Start with one section. If your list is weak, fix your list. If your messaging isn't getting responses, rewrite your emails using the framework above. If you're inconsistent, block 30 minutes tomorrow morning and protect that time.

The best time to build a prospecting system was five years ago. The second best time is this week.
