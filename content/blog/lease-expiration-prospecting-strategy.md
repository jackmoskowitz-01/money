# Lease Expiration Prospecting: The Complete Strategy for Tenant Rep Brokers

Every tenant rep broker knows the feeling: you close a deal, collect the commission, and then stare at an empty pipeline. The feast-or-famine cycle is the defining challenge of tenant representation, and the brokers who escape it all have one thing in common. They prospect lease expirations systematically, not sporadically.

Lease expiration prospecting is the single most reliable way to build a consistent book of business. A company approaching the end of its lease has a decision to make — renew, relocate, expand, or downsize — and they need guidance to make it well. Your job is to be the broker who shows up with that guidance before the landlord shows up with a renewal offer.

This guide covers the full workflow: where to find lease expiration data, how to build and prioritize your pipeline, the outreach sequence that converts, and the mistakes that cost brokers deals every quarter.

## Why Lease Expirations Are Your Best Lead Source

Not all prospects are created equal. A business owner who just signed a ten-year lease is not thinking about real estate. A company twelve months from expiration absolutely is — or will be very soon.

Lease expirations are high-intent signals. Unlike cold outreach to random businesses, you are reaching someone who has an actual, time-bound decision ahead of them. The space question is coming whether they want it or not. That changes the dynamic of every conversation.

Consider the timeline from a tenant's perspective. At eighteen months out, leadership starts asking questions internally. Is this space still working? Are we growing? Could we be paying less? By twelve months, landlords are sending renewal proposals — often with terms that heavily favor the building owner. By six months, tenants without a broker are negotiating against themselves, accepting whatever the landlord puts in front of them.

Your window is that twelve-to-eighteen-month mark for mid-size and large tenants, and six-to-twelve months for smaller ones. That is when a broker's market knowledge, negotiation leverage, and transaction management skills are most valuable — and most appreciated.

The math supports this. If you work a market with 5,000 commercial tenants and average lease terms of five to seven years, roughly 700 to 1,000 leases expire in any given year. That is your addressable pipeline, refreshing annually, in a single market.

## Where to Find Lease Expiration Data

The quality of your prospecting depends entirely on the quality of your data. Here are the primary sources, ranked by depth and accessibility.

### CoStar and CompStak

These are the industry standards for lease comp and expiration data. CoStar provides building-level tenant information, lease terms, and expiration dates across most major markets. CompStak crowdsources lease comps from brokers and appraisers, often capturing deal-level detail that CoStar misses. Both require paid subscriptions, but for serious tenant rep brokers, the ROI is immediate. One closed deal pays for a year of access multiple times over.

### County Recorder and Public Records

Lease memorandums and estoppel certificates are recorded in many jurisdictions, making basic lease terms — including start dates and durations — available through county recorder offices. This data is free but manual to pull. It works best when you are targeting specific buildings or submarkets rather than casting a wide net.

### SEC Filings for Public Companies

Public companies are required to disclose material lease obligations in their 10-K and 10-Q filings under ASC 842. These disclosures include lease terms, square footage, and expiration schedules — sometimes broken down by property. If you work with larger tenants or target Fortune 1000 companies in your market, SEC filings are an underused goldmine. Search the EDGAR database for companies headquartered or operating in your market and pull their lease schedules directly.

### Direct Observation

Never underestimate what you can learn by driving your market. For-lease signs on a building tell you a landlord is losing tenants. Construction activity suggests expansions. A half-empty parking lot at a building that was full a year ago signals consolidation. These observations do not give you expiration dates, but they identify buildings in transition — and the tenants inside are worth a call.

### News and Press Releases

Company announcements about relocations, expansions, layoffs, and mergers all have real estate implications. Set up Google Alerts for your market, follow local business journals, and monitor press releases from major employers in your territory. A company announcing a hiring push is going to need space. A company announcing layoffs may want to shed it. Both are opportunities.

### Your Own Network and Past Clients

Your existing relationships are your highest-converting data source. Past clients whose leases you handled three, five, or seven years ago are approaching expiration again. Colleagues, property managers, and attorneys in your network hear about tenant movements early. Make it a habit to ask: "Who do you know that is thinking about their space right now?" The referral pipeline compounds over time.

## Building Your Lease Expiration Pipeline

Having data is not the same as having a pipeline. A pipeline is organized, prioritized, and actionable.

### Organize by Expiration Date

Your pipeline should be sorted by lease expiration date, not alphabetically or by company size. Create rolling views: expirations in 6 months, 6 to 12 months, 12 to 18 months, and 18 to 24 months. Each window requires a different outreach approach and urgency level. Update this monthly as new data comes in and prospects move through stages.

### Set Your Outreach Windows

The ideal first-touch timing depends on tenant size. For tenants above 10,000 square feet, start outreach at 18 months before expiration. These companies have longer decision cycles, more stakeholders, and more complex requirements. For tenants between 2,000 and 10,000 square feet, 12 months is the sweet spot. For smaller tenants under 2,000 square feet, 6 to 9 months works because their decisions move faster.

### Prioritize by Fit

Not every expiring lease deserves the same effort. Prioritize using three factors:

**Square footage.** Larger tenants mean larger commissions and more complex deals where your expertise creates genuine value. Weight these heavily.

**Industry and growth trajectory.** A growing tech company is more likely to need space advisory than a stable accounting firm that has been in the same suite for fifteen years. Look for industries expanding in your market.

**Likelihood to move.** Tenants in older buildings, tenants whose companies have outgrown their space, and tenants in submarkets with rising rents are all more likely to engage a broker. Tenants with below-market rents in Class A space are less likely to move but may still benefit from renegotiation.

### Create Prospect Tiers

Segment your pipeline into three tiers:

**A Prospects** are your ideal clients. Right size, right industry, right timing, and a clear reason they would benefit from representation. These get your most personalized outreach and follow-up.

**B Prospects** are good fits with one or two unknowns. Maybe the timing is slightly early, or you are unsure about their growth plans. These get solid outreach with a plan to learn more.

**C Prospects** are worth a touch but not a heavy investment. Smaller tenants, tenants unlikely to move, or tenants outside your core specialty. A well-crafted email or market report keeps you on their radar without consuming hours of your week.

## The Outreach Sequence for Lease Expiration Prospects

How you reach out matters as much as when. The biggest rule: never lead with "I know your lease is expiring." Even though that information may be available through public or commercial data sources, it feels surveillance-like to the recipient. It puts them on the defensive instead of opening a conversation.

### First Touch: Lead with Market Knowledge

Your opening should deliver value, not announce that you have been tracking their lease. Position yourself as someone who knows the market and wants to share what you are seeing.

For an A Prospect, that might look like: "I have been tracking the [submarket] office market closely this quarter and noticed some shifts that could affect companies in your space. Landlords in your area are offering concessions we haven't seen in two years, and a few new options have come on the market that could be worth knowing about — whether you are evaluating your options or staying put. Happy to share what I am seeing if it is useful."

For a B or C Prospect, a market report or brief insight email works well: "I put together a quick snapshot of lease rates and availability in [submarket] for Q1. Thought it might be relevant given your location. Here is the link — let me know if any questions come up."

Neither message mentions the lease expiration. Both establish expertise and offer value.

### Follow-Up Cadence

Lease expiration prospects are not one-touch conversions. Your follow-up cadence should align with the expiration timeline:

**18 months out:** First touch plus two follow-ups over the next 60 days. Share market insights, a relevant case study, or a submarket report. The goal is awareness and credibility.

**12 months out:** Increase frequency. A direct call, a specific market comp, or a short note referencing a trigger event (new building announced, major tenant relocation in their area). The goal is engagement.

**9 months out:** This is your conversion window. If they have not responded, a more direct message works: "Most companies your size start evaluating options about a year out to make sure they have leverage in their renewal or relocation. I would be glad to run a quick market analysis so you can see where you stand — no commitment, just information."

**6 months out:** If they are still unresponsive, one final outreach noting that the window for maximum optionality is closing. After this, you are either engaged or you move on.

Throughout this sequence, mix channels. Email, phone, LinkedIn, and even a mailed market report. Different people respond to different formats.

## Common Mistakes in Lease Expiration Prospecting

Avoiding these puts you ahead of most of your competition.

**Starting too late.** By six months before expiration, landlords have already sent renewal proposals. The tenant feels locked in. Your leverage — and their perception of your value — drops significantly. If you are not reaching out at twelve months or earlier, you are playing catch-up.

**Leading with the expiration date.** As covered above, telling a prospect "I see your lease expires in March 2027" is the fastest way to end a conversation before it starts. It sounds like a sales pitch built on surveillance. Lead with market knowledge, not personal data.

**Not having a market story ready.** When a prospect does engage, you need a point of view. What is happening with rents in their submarket? Where are concessions trending? What buildings are coming online? If you cannot answer these questions fluently, you are not ready to prospect. Do the market research first, then pick up the phone.

**Ignoring renewals as opportunities.** Many brokers only pursue tenants they think will relocate, dismissing renewals as non-events. This is a mistake. Renewal negotiations are real engagements with real commissions. A tenant renewing a 15,000-square-foot lease with better terms, tenant improvement dollars, and reduced escalations is a full-scope representation assignment. Position yourself as the person who ensures they do not leave money on the table, whether they stay or go.

**Treating every prospect the same.** A Fortune 500 regional office and a local insurance agency should not receive the same email template. Tiering your prospects and tailoring your outreach — even modestly — dramatically improves response rates.

## Scaling Lease Expiration Prospecting

The workflow above works. The problem is time. Between servicing active clients, touring spaces, and negotiating deals, most brokers cannot maintain the volume of outreach that lease expiration prospecting demands. The math is simple: if you have 200 prospects in your pipeline and each needs five to eight touches over 12 months, that is over a thousand touchpoints to plan, write, and send — on top of everything else.

The brokers adopting these tools are not replacing the relationship — they are making sure it gets started in the first place.
