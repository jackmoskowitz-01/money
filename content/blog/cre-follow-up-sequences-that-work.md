# Follow-Up Sequences That Don't Annoy: A CRE Broker's Guide

Every tenant rep broker has the same quiet fear: that one more email will cross the line from professional persistence into nuisance territory. That you're one touchpoint away from becoming "that broker."

So you send one email, maybe two, and then you back off. You tell yourself you're being respectful. You move on to the next prospect and repeat the cycle.

Here's the problem: while you're being polite, another broker is on touch number four with that same prospect — and they're the one getting the meeting.

The line between persistent and annoying is thinner than you think. But it's learnable. It comes down to one principle: every follow-up either adds value or subtracts credibility. There is no neutral.

---

## The Data: Why Follow-Up Matters More Than You Think

**80% of commercial real estate deals require between 5 and 12 touches before a prospect engages.** The deals you're closing on the first or second touch are low-hanging fruit — the prospect who was already looking. Those are great. They're also rare.

The painful part: **the average tenant rep broker sends 1.2 outreach attempts per prospect.** One email and a partial follow-up. The gap between what's required and what's happening is the single biggest source of lost revenue in most brokerages.

The response rate data tells the story:

- **Touch 1:** ~5% response rate
- **Touch 2:** ~8% response rate
- **Touch 3:** ~12% response rate
- **Touch 4:** ~14% response rate
- **Touch 5:** ~11% response rate (still higher than touch 1)

Response rates *increase* through the first four touches. If you're stopping after one or two attempts, you're quitting right before the sequence starts working.

Here's what most brokers miss: **the majority of non-responses aren't rejection.** When a VP of Operations doesn't reply, they're almost never thinking "I don't want to hear from this person." They're thinking "I'm in back-to-back meetings" or "I'll deal with this next quarter." Most prospects aren't saying "no" — they're saying "not right now." Your job is to be there when "not right now" becomes "actually, yes."

---

## The Golden Rule: Add Value Every Touch

**Every follow-up must give the prospect a reason to engage that has nothing to do with your desire for their business.** Compare these two messages:

**Bad:** "Hi Sarah, just checking in to see if you had a chance to review my last email. Would love to connect when you have a moment."

**Good:** "Hi Sarah, two new 15,000 SF sublease availabilities just hit the Buckhead submarket this week — both below asking rate for the area. Given your lease timeline, these could be worth a look. Happy to send details."

The first message is about you. The second is about them — it positions you as a resource, not a salesperson. If you can't articulate the specific value in a given follow-up, don't send it.

Your arsenal of value-adds to rotate through:

- **Market data.** Vacancy rates, rental trends, absorption numbers for their submarket.
- **Relevant comps.** Recent lease transactions for similar companies in their area.
- **Industry news.** Competitor expansions, consolidation, regulatory changes affecting space needs.
- **Case studies.** How you helped a similar company save 18% on their renewal.
- **Market reports.** Quarterly snapshots, sublease availability summaries, construction pipeline updates.

Stack enough of these over a few weeks, and you've built more credibility than a dozen "just checking in" messages ever could.

## The 5-Touch Sequence (With Templates)

Here's a complete 5-touch sequence for tenant rep outreach. Each touch uses a different angle and, where possible, a different channel. Adapt these to your market and your prospect's situation.

### Touch 1 — Day 0: The Opening Email

**Goal:** Establish relevance with a market insight tied to their situation.

**Subject:** Quick note on [Submarket] lease rates

> Hi [First Name],
>
> I've been tracking the [Submarket] office market this quarter — asking rates on Class A space have dropped about 8% since Q3, and concession packages are the most aggressive I've seen in three years. For tenants with leases expiring in the next 12-18 months, the window to lock in favorable terms is real.
>
> I noticed [Company Name] has been at [Address/Building] for a while. If your lease is coming up, a preliminary market scan could give you leverage in a renewal conversation — or show you what's available if relocation makes sense.
>
> Happy to put something together. No strings attached.
>
> Best,
> [Your Name] | [Brokerage] | [Phone]

**Why it works:** You're leading with a market insight, not a pitch. The low-commitment offer lowers the barrier to response.

### Touch 2 — Day 4: LinkedIn Connection

**Goal:** Open a second channel and demonstrate genuine interest in their work.

Send a LinkedIn connection request with a personalized note:

> Hi [First Name] — I work with companies in [Submarket/Market] on their office strategy. I saw your recent [post about expansion / company announcement / article]. Thought it'd be great to connect.

Then engage with one of their recent posts — like it, leave a thoughtful comment, or share it. This is about showing up as a real person who pays attention.

**Why it works:** LinkedIn is a warmer channel than email. A connection request paired with genuine engagement makes you a face and a name. When your next email arrives, they'll recognize you.

### Touch 3 — Day 10: Second Email (New Angle)

**Goal:** Different type of value. If Touch 1 was market-level data, Touch 3 goes specific.

**Subject:** [Comp or data point] — thought this was relevant

> Hi [First Name],
>
> Wanted to pass along a comp. [Similar Company] just signed a [size] SF lease at [Building/Submarket] — similar industry to [Company Name], and they locked in a rate about 12% below what most tenants are paying in that corridor. The deal included 8 months free rent and a $65/SF TI package.
>
> Comps like this set the benchmark for what landlords are willing to offer. If [Company Name] ends up negotiating a renewal or exploring options, this kind of data changes the conversation.
>
> I track these deals as they happen. Happy to keep you in the loop.
>
> [Your Name]

**Why it works:** You've shifted from broad trends to a specific data point prospects can't easily get on their own.

### Touch 4 — Day 17: Phone Call

**Goal:** Break through the digital noise with a direct, brief conversation.

Keep it under 90 seconds. Script:

> "Hi [First Name], this is [Your Name] with [Brokerage]. I sent you a couple of emails about the [Submarket] market — I know those can get buried, so I wanted to put a voice to the name. I've been tracking some movement in your area and think there's an opportunity for [Company Name]. Do you have 10 minutes this week, or is there someone else on your team who handles real estate decisions?"

If you get voicemail:

> "Hi [First Name], [Your Name] with [Brokerage]. I've sent over some market data on [Submarket] relevant to [Company Name] — vacancy is shifting and there are opportunities worth flagging. My number is [Phone]. No pressure — just wanted to make sure it landed."

**Why it works:** A phone call after two well-crafted emails feels like a natural escalation, not a cold call. Referencing previous touchpoints shows consistency, and offering an easy out respects their time.

### Touch 5 — Day 28: The Break-Up Email

**Goal:** Close the sequence gracefully while leaving the door wide open.

**Subject:** Closing the loop

> Hi [First Name],
>
> I've reached out a few times about the [Submarket] market and opportunities that might be relevant to [Company Name]. I don't want to crowd your inbox, so this will be my last note for now.
>
> If your lease situation isn't a priority right now, I completely understand — timing is everything in this business. I'll continue tracking the market in your area, and if anything significant shifts, I'll send it your way.
>
> Whenever the timing is right — whether that's next month or next year — I'm an easy call to make.
>
> Best,
> [Your Name]
> [Phone]

**Why it works:** The break-up email is surprisingly effective. It signals social awareness, removes all pressure, and positions you as patient and professional. Many brokers report it generates more responses than any other touch in the sequence — prospects feel safe replying because you've told them you're not going to keep pushing.

## Channel Mix: Why Multi-Channel Wins

The numbers make this clear:

- **Email only:** ~8% average response rate
- **Email + phone:** ~15% average response rate
- **Email + phone + LinkedIn:** ~22% average response rate

Nearly three times the response rate by showing up in more than one place. Different people check different channels — the CFO who ignores cold emails might spend 30 minutes a day on LinkedIn.

Multi-channel reinforcement builds familiarity. When a prospect sees your name across channels within a few weeks, you stop being a stranger. The key: each channel should feel native, not forced. The prospect should never feel like they're being "sequenced."

---

## When to Stop (and What to Do After)

After five touches with no response, stop the active sequence. Continuing at the same pace will cost you credibility and may get your domain flagged.

But stopping doesn't mean giving up. It means shifting to **quarterly nurture.**

### The Quarterly Nurture Cadence

Every 90 days, send one piece of genuine value:

- **Q1:** Market report — vacancy trends, rate movements, notable transactions in their submarket.
- **Q2:** Relevant deal or case study — "We helped [similar company] save $X on their renewal."
- **Q3:** Industry insight — "Saw that [trend] is affecting companies in your space. Happy to share our report."
- **Q4:** Annual check-in — "Market's shifting heading into next year. If your lease is getting closer, it's worth starting early."

Over 12-18 months, you've sent four pieces of useful information. When their lease comes up, you're not a stranger — you're the broker who's been quietly helpful.

### Trigger-Based Re-Engagement

Some events should pull a prospect back into an active sequence immediately:

- **Funding round.** Growth capital means headcount growth, which means space needs change.
- **Leadership change.** A new CFO or COO often reassesses vendor relationships and real estate commitments.
- **Lease expiration approaching.** Within 12-18 months of expiration is a restart trigger.
- **Company in the news.** Layoffs, expansions, competitor relocations — anything signaling a shift in space needs.

When a trigger fires, restart the 5-touch sequence with messaging that references it. This isn't repetitive — it's timely.

## Systematizing Follow-Up

Everything above is useless if it lives in your head. The brokers who execute multi-touch sequences at scale build systems around them.

### CRM Reminders and Task Automation

Your CRM should generate your daily follow-up task list automatically. Send Touch 1 and the system creates tasks for every subsequent touch. You should never have to remember who needs a follow-up.

### Template Libraries With Customization Fields

Build a library organized by sequence stage and value-add type, with clearly marked customization fields. The template gets you 80% there — the customization is what makes it land. Without customization, it's just a mass email with your name on it.

## Putting It All Together

Follow-up isn't a personality trait. It's a system. The brokers who are consistently in front of the right prospects at the right time have better processes.

1. **Commit to five touches minimum** before you write off any prospect.
2. **Build around value**, not reminders. Every message should give the prospect something.
3. **Use multiple channels.** Email, phone, and LinkedIn together outperform any single channel by a wide margin.
4. **Know when to shift gears.** After five touches, move to quarterly nurture. Watch for triggers to restart.
5. **Systematize everything.** If follow-up depends on your memory, it won't happen.

The line between persistent and annoying isn't about frequency. It's about whether each touch makes the prospect's life slightly better or slightly worse. Get that right, and you can follow up as much as you need to.
