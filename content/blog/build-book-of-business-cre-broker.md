# How to Build a Book of Business as a CRE Broker (Even Starting from Zero)

There's a version of commercial real estate that gets talked about at conferences and on LinkedIn — the version where brokers close $50M deals, collect six-figure commissions, and make it look effortless.

Then there's the version nobody talks about. The version where you're six months in, you haven't closed a deal, your savings account is shrinking, and you're starting to wonder if you made a terrible career decision.

Both versions are real. The difference between the brokers who live the first version and the ones who wash out? It almost never comes down to talent, market knowledge, or connections. It comes down to whether they built the right habits and systems in the first two years — before the big deals showed up.

This guide is for the broker who's somewhere between month one and year five. Maybe you're just getting started. Maybe you've closed a few deals but can't figure out how to build consistent pipeline. Either way, here's the playbook that the top producers followed before they were top producers.

## The Reality of Year One

Let's get the uncomfortable truth out of the way: year one in CRE brokerage is brutal.

You likely have no pipeline, no relationships with decision-makers, and no reputation in your market. Your brokerage gave you a desk, maybe some training, and access to CoStar. The rest is on you.

The industry stats are sobering. Roughly 80% of new commercial real estate brokers leave the business within three years. Not because they weren't smart or hardworking — most of them were both. They left because they underestimated how long it takes to build a revenue-generating book of business and ran out of runway before the pipeline kicked in.

The brokers who survive year one — and eventually thrive — all share the same trait. They didn't wait for deals to find them. They prospected relentlessly and built repeatable systems from day one. They treated their first year not as a slow ramp-up, but as an outreach sprint. While other new brokers spent months "learning the market" or waiting for their senior broker to hand them a lead, the survivors were making calls, sending emails, and building a database.

Here's the mindset shift: in year one, your job isn't to close deals. Your job is to fill the pipeline that closes deals in year two. Every conversation, every email, every coffee meeting is an investment in future revenue. If you accept that timeline and plan your finances accordingly, you've already separated yourself from most of your peers.

## Pick Your Lane

One of the biggest mistakes new brokers make is trying to be everything to everyone. "I do commercial real estate" sounds reasonable, but it's a recipe for mediocrity.

The brokers who build books of business fastest are the ones who specialize early. They pick a lane — a specific intersection of submarket, asset class, and client profile — and they go deep.

Here's the difference in practice:

**Generic:** "I'm a commercial real estate broker in Dallas."

**Specific:** "I help tech and SaaS companies between 50 and 500 employees find office space in Uptown and the Design District."

The specific broker wins every time. Why? Because specialization creates compounding advantages:

- **You learn faster.** When you focus on one submarket and one asset class, you know every building, every landlord, every comp. You become genuinely expert in months instead of years.
- **You become referable.** When a startup founder asks their attorney "Do you know a good commercial real estate person?" the attorney won't remember the generalist. They'll remember the broker who specifically helps tech companies find office space.
- **Your outreach is sharper.** When you know your niche cold, your emails and calls demonstrate real expertise. You can reference specific buildings, market trends, and comparable transactions. That's the difference between a cold email that gets deleted and one that gets a reply.

Picking your lane doesn't mean turning away business. If an industrial deal lands in your lap, take it. But your proactive prospecting — the work that builds your book — should be focused. You can always expand your lane later. Right now, own a corner of the market so thoroughly that you become the obvious choice.

## The 100-Company Target List

Every serious book of business starts with a list. Not a vague mental list of "companies I should call." A real, researched, documented list of 100 companies that fit your ideal client profile.

This is your foundation, and it's worth spending real time on.

For each company on your list, you should know:

- **Lease situation.** When does their current lease expire? Are they in a sublease? Have they outgrown their space? A company with 18 months left on their lease is in your sweet spot — early enough that they haven't started looking, late enough that the decision is on the horizon.
- **Growth trajectory.** Are they hiring? Have they raised funding recently? Are they expanding into your market? Growth signals tell you whether they'll need more space, different space, or a new market entirely.
- **Decision-maker.** Who makes the real estate call? In a 50-person company, it might be the CEO or COO. In a 500-person company, it's likely a VP of Operations or Head of Real Estate. Know the name before you pick up the phone.
- **Relevant context.** What's happening in their industry? Have they been in the news? Is their current building being sold or repositioned? The more context you bring to the first conversation, the more you sound like someone worth talking to.

Where do you find these 100 companies? Start with CoStar and your local business journal. Layer in LinkedIn Sales Navigator, Crunchbase (for tech companies), and local economic development announcements. Ask your senior brokers who's active in your submarket. Check building directories in your target area.

The list isn't static. It's a living document. Companies will fall off — they renewed early, they got acquired, their decision-maker left. New companies will earn a spot. But the discipline of maintaining a curated, researched target list is what separates brokers who prospect from brokers who just make random calls.

## The First 90 Days: Outreach Sprint

You have your lane. You have your list. Now it's time to execute. Here's how to structure your first 90 days of outreach.

### Weeks 1-2: Build and Research

Dedicate these two weeks entirely to building your 100-company list and completing the research on each one. Yes, this feels like you're "not doing anything" because you're not reaching out yet. Resist the urge to start cold calling with half-baked research. The quality of your list determines the quality of your pipeline.

By the end of week two, you should have 100 companies with complete profiles — lease expiration, growth signals, decision-maker name and contact info, and at least one personalized angle for your first touch.

### Weeks 3-4: First Touch

Now you go. Every company on your list gets a first touch — a personalized email, a LinkedIn message, or a phone call. Not all three at once. Pick your primary channel (email works best for most brokers starting out) and hit all 100.

These emails should not be generic. Each one should reference something specific about the company — their growth, their lease timeline, a trend in their industry, a building you know they should consider. If your email could be sent to any company by swapping the name, it's not good enough. For frameworks that actually work, check out our [cold email templates for CRE brokers](/blog/commercial-real-estate-cold-email-templates).

### Weeks 5-8: The Follow-Up Sequence

Here's where most new brokers fail. They send one email, get no response, and assume the prospect isn't interested. In reality, the average cold email response rate in B2B is somewhere around 3-5% on the first touch. The magic happens in the follow-up.

Build a follow-up sequence: touch two at one week, touch three at two weeks, touch four at four weeks. Each follow-up should add new value — a market update, a relevant comp, an article about their industry, an invitation to a local event. You're not "checking in." You're demonstrating consistent expertise over time.

During these four weeks, you should also be calling. Not every prospect on every touch — but the ones who opened your email, visited your LinkedIn, or showed any signal of engagement. The phone is still the fastest path to a real conversation.

### Weeks 9-12: Refresh and Expand

By now, your original 100 have been touched three to four times. Some have responded — great, move them into active conversations. Some have explicitly said no — remove them and replace them. Some have gone silent — keep them in a long-term nurture cadence.

Add 50 new companies to your list. Apply the same research rigor and outreach sequence. You're building the habit of always having fresh prospects entering the top of your funnel.

**Your 90-day target:** 10 to 15 real conversations with decision-makers. Not "meetings booked" or "emails sent" — actual conversations where a prospect engaged with you about their space needs. If you hit that number, you're ahead of most brokers with five years of experience. ## Building Your Reputation (While You Wait for Deals)

Here's the paradox of year one: you need a reputation to win deals, but you need deals to build a reputation. The workaround is to build your brand through expertise and visibility, even before you've closed your first transaction.

### Publish Market Intelligence

You don't need a research department to produce valuable market content. Start simple. Write a quarterly submarket report for your lane — vacancy rates, notable transactions, asking rents, new developments. Pull the data from CoStar, talk to a few landlord brokers, and put it in a one-page PDF. Send it to your prospect list and post it on LinkedIn.

This does two things: it forces you to actually learn your market deeply, and it positions you as someone who understands the data, not just someone trying to get a commission.

### Show Up on LinkedIn

Post consistently. Two to three times per week is plenty. Share market observations, comment on industry news, break down a deal (with permission or from public records), and offer practical insights. You don't need to go viral. You need the 200 people in your submarket to see your name regularly and associate it with expertise.

### Be Physically Present

Attend every industry event in your submarket — NAIOP chapter meetings, SIOR events, local CRE networking breakfasts, ULI panels. Go to your city's tech meetups, startup events, or industry gatherings relevant to your target clients. Don't go to sell. Go to learn and be seen. The relationships you build at these events will turn into referrals, co-brokerage opportunities, and market intelligence.

### Offer Value Before Asking for Business

Offer free lease reviews to companies on your target list. "I noticed your lease at 100 Main Street expires in 14 months — I put together a quick overview of what the market looks like for your size range in the submarket. No strings attached." This is not desperate. This is the highest-converting prospecting tactic in tenant rep. You're leading with value and demonstrating exactly the expertise they'd be hiring you for.

## The Referral Engine

The best brokers don't just prospect tenants. They build a network of professionals who send tenants to them. This is your referral engine, and building it early is one of the highest-leverage things you can do.

Think about who interacts with companies before, during, or after a real estate decision:

- **Real estate attorneys** — They handle lease negotiations. They know when clients are thinking about space.
- **CPAs and financial advisors** — They see the rent line on the P&L. They know when a client is spending too much or planning to expand.
- **Architects and interior designers** — They get called when a company is planning a buildout, often before a broker is involved.
- **General contractors** — Same as above. They hear about space needs early.
- **Business insurance brokers** — They insure the spaces. They know who's moving, expanding, or consolidating.

Build relationships with three to five professionals in each category. Take them to coffee. Refer your clients to them. Share market intelligence with them. When you send them business, they send you business — and their referrals come with built-in trust because a trusted advisor made the introduction.

The key principle: **give referrals to get referrals.** Every time you close a deal or have a conversation with a tenant, ask yourself who else they might need — an attorney, a contractor, a furniture dealer — and make the introduction. This creates a cycle of reciprocity that compounds over years.

## From One Deal to a Pipeline

Your first closed deal changes everything. Not because of the commission check (though that helps), but because it gives you proof. You've done the thing. You can talk about it specifically, credibly, and with the confidence that comes from experience.

Here's how to think about the progression:

**Deal 1** teaches you the process. You learn the timeline, the negotiation dynamics, the paperwork, the pitfalls. You make mistakes and figure things out. Most importantly, you have a client who can vouch for you.

**Deal 2** proves it wasn't a fluke. Now you have a pattern, not an anecdote. Your confidence on prospecting calls increases measurably because you've been through the cycle.

**By deal 5,** you have a portfolio. You have case studies you can share, testimonials you can reference, and specific comps you can cite in prospect conversations. You're no longer selling a promise — you're selling a track record.

The discipline that separates good brokers from great ones: **use every closed deal to generate future business.** After every transaction, you should:

- Ask for **three to five referrals**. "Who else in your network is thinking about their space?" Works best when asked within a week of closing, when goodwill is highest.
- Write up **one case study**. It doesn't need to be fancy. The problem, your approach, the result. Post it on LinkedIn. Add it to your pitch deck.
- Send a **closing announcement** to your entire prospect list and referral network. People pay attention to closed deals — it's social proof that you're active and capable.
- Reconnect with your **referral partners** who were involved and strengthen those relationships.

If every deal generates three referrals and one piece of content, your pipeline builds itself over time. The math gets very favorable very quickly.

## Scaling Past the Plateau

Here's something nobody warns you about: there's a ceiling that almost every broker hits. It usually shows up around $300K to $500K in gross commission income. You're busy. You're closing deals. But you can't seem to break through to the next level.

The reason is simple: you've maxed out your manual capacity. There are only so many prospects you can research, so many emails you can write, so many follow-ups you can track in your head or your spreadsheet. The activities that got you to $400K — raw hustle, personal outreach, relationship-driven prospecting — don't scale linearly. You can't work twice as hard to earn twice as much, because you're already working at capacity.

The brokers who break through the plateau all do the same thing: they build systems.

**CRM discipline.** If you're still tracking prospects in a spreadsheet or your inbox, you're leaking opportunities. A real CRM — even a simple one — ensures no prospect falls through the cracks, follow-ups happen on schedule, and you can see your pipeline at a glance.

**Email sequences.** The follow-up cadence you ran manually in your first 90 days? Automate it. Set up sequences that nurture your prospect list with market intelligence, relevant content, and check-ins — so you're staying in front of hundreds of prospects, not just the 20 you can manually track.

**AI-assisted prospecting.** This is where the leverage really multiplies. The research that took you 30 minutes per prospect — lease expirations, growth signals, decision-maker identification, personalized outreach angles — can now be done at scale with the right tools. Brokers who adopt AI-assisted prospecting aren't replacing what got them here. They're multiplying it. They're reaching 500 prospects with the same personalized, research-backed approach they used to reach 50.

The brokers building $1M+ books of business in 2026 aren't working ten times harder than everyone else. They're working the same hours with systems that multiply their reach, their consistency, and their ability to be in the right conversation at the right time.

## The Bottom Line

Building a book of business in CRE is not complicated. It's also not easy. The playbook is straightforward — specialize, build your list, prospect relentlessly, add value before asking for business, build a referral network, turn every deal into future pipeline, and invest in systems before you think you need them.

The hard part is doing it consistently for 18 to 24 months before the compound effects kick in. Most brokers quit during that window. The ones who don't are the ones collecting those six-figure commission checks five years from now.

You don't need a fancy pedigree, a massive network, or a senior broker handing you leads. You need a lane, a list, a system, and the discipline to show up every day and work the process.

Start with your 100 companies. Start this week.
