# Commercial Real Estate Cold Email Templates That Actually Get Responses

Most commercial real estate cold emails get deleted within two seconds. And honestly, most of them deserve it.

If your outreach reads like a press release about your brokerage's "full-service platform" and "decades of combined experience," you've already lost. Decision-makers at companies leasing 5,000 to 500,000 square feet of space get hammered with generic broker pitches. They're not reading yours unless you give them a reason to.

I've spent years in tenant rep, and the emails that actually get responses share three traits: they're specific, they're relevant to the recipient's situation right now, and they're short. That's it. No magic. Just discipline.

Below are seven templates I've used (or seen top-producing brokers use) in real prospecting workflows. Each one is tied to a specific scenario, because the scenario is what makes the email feel real instead of mass-produced. I've also included sections on deliverability — because the best email in the world is worthless if it lands in spam — and on what makes CRE outreach fundamentally different from residential.

Copy these. Modify them. Make them yours. But more importantly, understand *why* each one works so you can adapt when the situation calls for it.

---

## What Makes CRE Cold Emails Different From Residential

Before we get into templates, let's address something that trips up a lot of brokers, especially those who came up in residential or who hire marketing people from outside the industry.

**Commercial real estate outreach is not residential outreach.** The differences matter:

- **Longer decision timelines.** A tenant evaluating a 10-year lease on 40,000 SF of office space is not making a snap decision. Your email is planting a seed that might not sprout for 6-18 months. That changes the tone entirely — you're not creating urgency, you're establishing credibility.

- **Multiple stakeholders.** You're often emailing a CFO, VP of Real Estate, or Head of Operations. These people answer to boards and committees. Your email needs to be something they'd feel comfortable forwarding to their boss, not something that reads like a used-car pitch.

- **The deals are larger and fewer.** In residential, volume is the game. In CRE tenant rep, one relationship can be worth mid-six-figures in commission over a few years. That means your emails should feel like the start of a relationship, not a transaction.

- **Market knowledge is your currency.** Residential buyers want to know you'll hold their hand through the process. Commercial tenants want to know you understand their submarket, their competitors, and their leverage. Lead with insight, not with service descriptions.

- **Compliance matters more.** CAN-SPAM applies to everyone, but in CRE you're emailing C-suite professionals who will absolutely report you as spam if you're sloppy. One-click unsubscribe, real physical address, honest subject lines — these aren't optional.

Bottom line: every template below is written for a professional audience that values their time, expects you to have done your homework, and will judge your competence by how you communicate before you ever meet them.

## The 7 Templates

### Template 1: Lease Expiration Trigger

**The Scenario**

You've identified that a company's lease is expiring in the next 12-24 months. Maybe you pulled it from CoStar, a county records search, or your own database. This is the single highest-intent trigger in tenant rep prospecting. The company *will* need to make a decision — renew, relocate, or renegotiate — and the clock is ticking.

**The Email**

> **Subject:** Your lease at 400 Meridian Pkwy
>
> Hi Karen,
>
> I noticed that DataCore Solutions' lease at 400 Meridian Parkway is coming up in early 2027. Depending on your plans, that puts you in a window right now where the negotiating leverage is strongest — landlords in the Plano corridor are sitting at 22% vacancy and competing hard for credit tenants.
>
> I represent tenants exclusively in the DFW market and recently helped a similar-sized tech company at Legacy West negotiate a 14-month free rent package on their renewal. Happy to share what I'm seeing in the market if it's useful.
>
> Would a 15-minute call this week or next make sense?
>
> Best,
> Ryan Torres
> Apex Tenant Advisors
> 214-555-0178

**Why It Works**

You've demonstrated three things in under 100 words: you know their specific situation, you know the market, and you've delivered results for someone like them. The recipient doesn't have to wonder *why* you're emailing — the reason is obvious and timely.

**Personalization Tips**

- Always include the actual property address. It proves you're not blasting a list.
- Reference a real market stat (vacancy rate, average asking rent, concession trends). It takes 5 minutes to look up and separates you from 95% of brokers.
- The comparable deal ("similar-sized tech company") is powerful. If you can make it more specific without violating confidentiality, do it.
- If the lease expiration is more than 18 months out, soften the ask — offer a market report instead of a call.

### Template 2: Market Update / Value-Add Intro

**The Scenario**

You don't have a specific trigger, but you want to introduce yourself to a prospect by leading with value. This works well for companies you've identified as ideal clients — right size, right industry, right submarket — but where you don't know their exact lease status.

**The Email**

> **Subject:** Q1 industrial lease rates in South Atlanta — worth a look
>
> Hi Marcus,
>
> I put together a quick breakdown of where industrial lease rates landed in the South Atlanta / Henry County corridor this quarter. The short version: asking rates are up 8% YoY, but effective rates are actually flat once you factor in the TI packages landlords are throwing in to keep buildings stabilized.
>
> For a distribution operation your size, that gap creates real opportunity if you're evaluating space in the next 12-18 months.
>
> I put the full analysis in a one-page PDF — happy to send it over if you're interested. No strings.
>
> Marcus, appreciate your time either way.
>
> Derek Chung
> Ridgeline CRE Partners
> 404-555-0291

**Why It Works**

You're leading with insight, not a sales pitch. The "no strings" line lowers the barrier. And by not attaching the PDF upfront, you create a reason for the prospect to reply — which is the real goal. A reply is worth more than a click.

**Personalization Tips**

- The market data must be real and current. Making up stats will destroy your credibility with anyone who knows the market.
- Tailor the submarket and asset class to the recipient's business. Don't send office stats to a logistics company.
- If you have a blog post or market report published on your site, you can link to it instead. This also establishes authority.
- Mention their company's size or industry to show you know who they are.

### Template 3: New to the Area / Expansion Signal

**The Scenario**

You've spotted a signal that a company is expanding into your market — a job posting in a new city, a press release about growth plans, a filing, or even a LinkedIn post from their leadership. This is gold, because the company has a real need and probably hasn't engaged a broker yet.

**The Email**

> **Subject:** Saw you're hiring in Austin — space question
>
> Hi Jennifer,
>
> I came across several engineering roles NovaBridge is posting in Austin. If that means you're looking at office or flex space in the market, I'd love to save you some time.
>
> Austin's a different animal than it was two years ago — sublease inventory in the Domain and East Side corridors has tightened significantly, but there are some off-market opportunities in the 8,000-15,000 SF range that haven't hit the listing platforms yet.
>
> I represent tenants only (never landlords), so my job is to make sure you see everything available and negotiate from the strongest position.
>
> Worth a quick conversation?
>
> Megan Clark
> Tidewater Advisory Group
> 512-555-0384

**Why It Works**

The subject line is specific and intriguing — it references something *they* did, which triggers curiosity. The "tenants only" line is a differentiator that matters to sophisticated occupiers. And the mention of off-market opportunities gives them a reason to engage that they can't get from a Google search.

**Personalization Tips**

- Link to the actual job posting or press release if possible. It shows your homework.
- Be specific about the size range you think they'd need. Even a rough estimate shows you're thinking about their situation, not yours.
- If you know what type of space they occupy in other markets, mention it. ("I saw your Denver office is in a creative flex building — we have some similar options here.")
- Time this email as close to the signal as possible. Two weeks after the job posting goes up, three other brokers have already emailed them.

### Template 4: Referral-Based Introduction

**The Scenario**

Someone in your network — a client, an attorney, a CPA, a contractor — has mentioned a company that might need tenant rep services. A warm intro is always better than a cold one, but sometimes the referrer doesn't want to make a formal introduction. In that case, you drop their name (with permission) and reach out directly.

**The Email**

> **Subject:** Tom Hendricks suggested I reach out
>
> Hi Diane,
>
> Tom Hendricks at Blackwell & Associates mentioned you're evaluating your office situation as the team grows. He thought it might be helpful to connect.
>
> I work with Tom on several of his clients' real estate needs in the Charlotte market. Most recently helped a 35-person professional services firm in SouthPark move from a full-floor lease they'd outgrown into a build-to-suit that actually matched their headcount projections.
>
> I'd be happy to share what I'm seeing in the market and give you a sense of your options — no commitment required. Would 15 minutes work sometime next week?
>
> David Orenstein
> Cornerstone Tenant Advisory
> 704-555-0112

**Why It Works**

The referrer's name in the subject line is the most reliable open-rate booster in existence. It transforms a cold email into a warm one. The rest of the email follows the same principles: specific example, relevant market, low-pressure ask.

**Personalization Tips**

- Always get permission before using someone's name. This sounds obvious, but brokers skip it all the time, and it can backfire badly.
- Briefly explain your relationship with the referrer so it doesn't feel manufactured.
- If the referrer gave you specific details about the prospect's situation, use them — but don't reveal anything the prospect might consider confidential.
- Follow up with the referrer afterward. Let them know you reached out and what happened. It keeps the referral pipeline open.

### Template 5: After a Networking Event / LinkedIn Connection

**The Scenario**

You met someone at a NAIOP event, a local business association mixer, or connected with them on LinkedIn. You had a brief interaction — maybe a 3-minute conversation or a comment exchange on a post — and now you want to turn it into a real conversation.

**The Email**

> **Subject:** Good meeting you at the BOMA luncheon
>
> Hi Sam,
>
> Glad we got a chance to talk at the BOMA luncheon on Tuesday. Your point about the challenge of planning office space around hybrid schedules stuck with me — I hear the same thing from almost every company in the 50-150 employee range right now.
>
> One thing I've been helping clients with is benchmarking their space utilization against what we're seeing in comparable companies across the Denver market. It's a simple analysis, but it's saved a few clients from either over-committing on space or locking into a lease that's too small 18 months in.
>
> If that's relevant to what Bridgepoint is working through, I'd be glad to share what the data looks like. Totally informal.
>
> Best,
> Nate Salazar
> Front Range Realty Advisors
> 303-555-0247

**Why It Works**

You've referenced a specific moment from a real interaction, which makes this feel like a genuine follow-up, not a template. You've then connected their stated challenge to a service you provide without making it feel like a pitch. The tone is peer-to-peer, not salesperson-to-prospect.

**Personalization Tips**

- Send this within 48 hours of the event. After that, the connection fades.
- Reference something specific they said. This is the single biggest differentiator between a follow-up that gets a reply and one that doesn't.
- If it was a LinkedIn connection, reference the post or comment that prompted the connection.
- Keep the ask small. You're not trying to close a deal — you're trying to start a conversation.

### Template 6: Follow-Up After No Response (2nd Touch)

**The Scenario**

You sent one of the emails above (or something similar) and got no response. This is normal. Most prospecting emails don't get a reply on the first attempt — not because the recipient isn't interested, but because they're busy and your email got buried. The second touch is where a lot of deals are actually born.

**The Email**

> **Subject:** Re: Your lease at 400 Meridian Pkwy
>
> Hi Karen,
>
> I sent a note a couple weeks ago about your lease coming up at 400 Meridian. I know these things land at busy times, so I wanted to circle back with one quick data point:
>
> Three tenants in the Plano corridor your size have renegotiated in the last 90 days, and the average concession package has been 11 months free rent + $45/SF in TI. That's a material improvement from six months ago, and I don't expect it to hold past Q3 as the pipeline fills.
>
> If the timing is right for a quick market briefing, I'm around this week. If not, no worries at all — happy to reconnect when it makes sense.
>
> Ryan Torres
> Apex Tenant Advisors
> 214-555-0178

**Why It Works**

The "Re:" subject line references the original thread, which signals continuity, not desperation. The email doesn't complain about not getting a reply — it adds new, valuable information. The time-sensitive data point creates mild urgency without being pushy. And the "no worries" exit ramp shows confidence.

**Personalization Tips**

- Wait 10-14 days between your first and second touch. Any sooner feels aggressive; any longer and they've forgotten the first email entirely.
- Always add new information. If your follow-up is just "checking in," you're wasting an at-bat.
- Reference a specific and timely data point — a recent comparable deal, a market shift, a new building delivery. This gives the email a reason to exist.
- Keep the same thread if possible. Replying to your original email keeps context intact and makes it easy for the recipient to scan the full picture.

### Template 7: Break-Up Email (Final Touch)

**The Scenario**

You've sent two or three emails with no response. Before you move this prospect to your long-term nurture list, you send one final email. Counterintuitively, the break-up email often gets the highest response rate in a sequence because it flips the dynamic — you're walking away, and that triggers loss aversion.

**The Email**

> **Subject:** Should I close the file?
>
> Hi Karen,
>
> I've reached out a couple of times about your upcoming lease decision at 400 Meridian and haven't heard back, so I'll assume the timing isn't right.
>
> I'll keep an eye on the Plano market on my end. If anything changes on yours — or if you just want a second opinion when the time comes — I'm easy to find.
>
> Either way, wishing you and the DataCore team a great rest of the year.
>
> Ryan Torres
> Apex Tenant Advisors
> 214-555-0178

**Why It Works**

It's short, gracious, and zero-pressure. The subject line — "Should I close the file?" — is one of the most reliable openers in outbound sales, across every industry. It implies finality, which prompts people to reply if there's even a sliver of interest. And it leaves the door open without begging.

**Personalization Tips**

- This should genuinely be your last proactive email for a while. If you send a "break-up" email and then follow up again two weeks later, you've destroyed your credibility.
- Keep it under 75 words. The brevity is part of the power.
- Don't introduce new information or new pitches here. The entire point is graceful closure.
- After sending this, move the prospect to a quarterly or semi-annual touchpoint — market reports, relevant articles, holiday notes. Stay visible without being intrusive.

## Email Deliverability Basics for CRE Brokers

You can write the perfect email, but if it lands in the spam folder or the promotions tab, nobody will ever see it. Deliverability is the unsexy foundation that makes everything else work. Here's what you need to know.

### Domain Reputation

Your email domain has a reputation score, and every email you send either builds it up or tears it down. If you're sending outreach from the same domain you use for client communication (e.g., ryan@apexadvisors.com), a bad reputation doesn't just kill your prospecting — it can put your regular business emails in spam too.

**What to do:**

- Consider using a separate domain for outreach (e.g., apexadvisors.co or outreach-apexadvisors.com) and warm it up before sending at volume.
- Set up SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records for any domain you send from. If those acronyms mean nothing to you, ask your IT person or email provider. These are non-negotiable for inbox placement.
- Monitor your domain reputation using free tools like Google Postmaster Tools or MXToolBox.

### Send Volume and Warm-Up

If you suddenly start sending 200 cold emails a day from an account that normally sends 15, email providers will flag you immediately. Ramp up gradually.

**What to do:**

- Start with 10-15 outbound emails per day from a new or underused domain.
- Increase by 5-10 per day each week, as long as your bounce rate and spam complaint rate stay low.
- Never send more than 50-80 cold emails per day per mailbox, even after warm-up. If you need higher volume, add more sending accounts.
- Spread sends throughout the day. Blasting 50 emails at 8:00 AM looks automated (because it is).

### List Hygiene

Sending emails to invalid addresses tanks your deliverability. Every hard bounce is a black mark.

**What to do:**

- Verify email addresses before sending using a tool like NeverBounce, ZeroBounce, or MillionVerifier.
- Remove anyone who hard bounces immediately.
- If your open rate drops below 20%, stop and diagnose before sending more.

### Content That Triggers Spam Filters

Certain patterns will get your emails filtered regardless of your domain reputation:

- **Avoid spam trigger words** in subject lines: "free," "guaranteed," "act now," "limited time." These are less of an issue in plain-text business emails, but they still matter.
- **Don't use link-heavy emails.** One or two links maximum. No images in cold outreach.
- **Write in plain text.** HTML templates with heavy formatting, colored fonts, and embedded images scream "marketing email." Your cold outreach should look like a normal email from a normal person — because that's what it is.
- **Personalize every email.** Identical content sent to hundreds of recipients is the textbook definition of spam to a filter. Even small variations in the opening line help.

### Compliance

This should go without saying, but:

- Include a physical mailing address in your email or signature.
- Provide an easy way to unsubscribe. A simple "If you'd prefer not to hear from me, just let me know and I'll remove you from my list" works fine for low-volume outreach.
- Honor opt-outs immediately and permanently.
- If you're emailing prospects in Canada (CASL) or the EU (GDPR), the rules are stricter. Know them before you send.

## The Metrics That Matter

Once you're sending consistently, track these numbers:

- **Open rate:** 40-60% is good for targeted CRE outreach. Below 30%, you have a subject line or deliverability problem.
- **Reply rate:** 5-15% is solid. Above 15%, you're doing something right. Below 5%, revisit your targeting or messaging.
- **Bounce rate:** Keep this under 3%. Above that, your list needs cleaning.
- **Positive reply rate:** Not all replies are created equal. Track how many replies are genuinely interested vs. "please remove me." The ratio tells you whether your targeting is sharp or sloppy.

Don't obsess over open rates — they're increasingly unreliable due to Apple's Mail Privacy Protection and similar tools. Reply rate is the number that actually correlates with pipeline.

## Putting It All Together: A Sample 3-Touch Sequence

Here's how a complete outreach sequence might look for a lease expiration trigger:

**Day 1:** Send Template 1 (Lease Expiration Trigger). Personalized with property address, market stats, and a relevant comparable.

**Day 12:** No reply. Send Template 6 (Follow-Up). New data point about recent deals in their submarket. Same thread.

**Day 26:** Still no reply. Send Template 7 (Break-Up). Short, gracious, door left open.

**After Day 26:** Move to long-term nurture. Quarterly market updates, relevant articles, annual check-in around their lease milestone dates.

That's it. Three emails over about a month. No harassment, no daily "just checking in" messages, no desperate voicemails. Professional, persistent, and respectful of their time.

## Scaling Personalized Outreach Without Losing Quality

Here's the tension every tenant rep broker faces: the templates above work because they're specific and personal. But being specific and personal takes time. Writing a truly customized email — researching the prospect, pulling market data, finding the right angle — might take 15-20 minutes per contact. At that rate, you can reach maybe 15-20 prospects a day if prospecting is literally all you do. And it's not. You have tours to run, deals to negotiate, leases to review, and clients who need answers by 5 PM.

This is where most brokers hit a ceiling. They know what good outreach looks like, but they can't do it at the volume needed to consistently fill a pipeline. So they either default to generic blasts (which don't work) or they prospect inconsistently (which doesn't work either).

The brokers and teams producing at the highest level right now are using AI to close that gap. Not to write emails for them — the templates above prove that the thinking behind the email matters more than the words — but to handle the research, the personalization, and the sequencing at scale. Pulling lease expirations, matching them to market data, identifying the right contact, drafting a first version of the email, and scheduling the follow-up sequence — all of that can be automated intelligently so the broker's time is spent on what actually requires their judgment: the relationship.

If you're a tenant rep broker or brokerage leader who's leaving deals on the table because there simply aren't enough hours to reach every prospect who should be hearing from you, it might be worth exploring what that looks like with modern AI tools. The technology has caught up to the problem. The brokers who figure that out first will own their markets.

## Final Thoughts

Cold email in commercial real estate isn't about tricks or hacks. It's about reaching the right person with the right message at the right time — and doing it consistently. The templates in this post give you a framework, but the real work is in the customization: knowing your market cold, understanding your prospect's situation, and writing like a human being who has something genuinely useful to offer.

Start with one template. Customize it for five prospects this week. Send it. Track the results. Adjust. Do it again next week. The brokers who build this muscle — and keep building it — are the ones who never worry about where the next deal is coming from.
