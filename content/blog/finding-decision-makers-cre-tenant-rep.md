# Finding the Decision-Maker: Who to Contact for CRE Tenant Rep Deals

You spent 45 minutes researching the company, another 20 crafting a personalized email, and hit send with confidence. Three days later: nothing. A week: silence. You follow up. More silence. The email was good. The timing was right. The company actually needs space. So what went wrong?

You emailed the wrong person.

This is the single most expensive mistake in tenant rep prospecting, and nearly every brokerage makes it daily. The outreach itself is fine. The targeting is broken.

## Why the Wrong Contact Kills Your Deal

It sounds obvious, but it bears stating plainly: the best prospecting email ever written is worthless if it lands in the inbox of someone who has zero authority over where the company leases space.

Think about what happens when your email reaches the wrong person. Best case, they ignore it. Worst case, they forward it to the actual decision-maker with a dismissive note — "some broker emailed me, not sure why" — and you have now been introduced through the lens of irrelevance instead of expertise.

The math is brutal. If you are sending 50 outreach emails a week and even 40% are going to the wrong contact, that is 20 wasted touches per week. Over a quarter, that is 260 emails that never had a chance. At a conservative conversion rate, those misdirected emails could have represented two or three real conversations and at least one new engagement.

The fix is not sending more email. It is sending email to the right person. And that starts with understanding who actually makes real estate decisions at different types of companies.

## The Decision-Maker Landscape by Company Size

There is no single title that universally controls the real estate decision. The person you need changes dramatically based on company size, stage, and industry. Here is how it typically breaks down.

### Companies with 10-50 Employees

At this size, the CEO or founder makes the call directly. There is no real estate team. There is no facilities manager. The founder picked the current office, probably negotiated the lease themselves (or had their attorney do it), and will make the next decision the same way.

Sometimes a COO or operations lead is involved, but even then, the founder has final say. Your outreach should go directly to the top.

**Example:** A 30-person SaaS startup in Austin with a lease expiring in eight months. The CEO personally toured spaces last time. She is the only person who will respond to your email, and she will respond if you can articulate why this move matters for her hiring plan.

### Companies with 50-200 Employees

This is where it gets more complex. The CEO is still aware of real estate decisions but has likely delegated the process to someone else. The most common decision-makers at this stage are:

- **CFO:** Controls the budget and cares about lease economics
- **VP of Operations:** Manages the physical footprint as part of broader operational responsibilities
- **Head of People/HR:** Increasingly involved as companies tie office strategy to culture and recruiting

At many companies in this range, all three have a voice. But one of them owns the process. Your job is to figure out which one.

**Example:** A 120-person fintech company in Chicago. The CFO approved the last lease because it was a $2M annual commitment. The Head of People sourced options because they wanted a space that would help with recruiting. You need to reach the CFO for authority and the Head of People for influence.

### Companies with 200-1,000 Employees

Now you are dealing with companies that have formalized the real estate function, even if it is not a full department. Look for:

- **VP or Director of Real Estate:** This title exists at many companies in this range, even if the person also handles facilities or workplace strategy
- **VP of Facilities:** Manages the physical space and often drives the site selection process
- **CFO:** Still involved at the approval level, especially for headquarters or major leases

The key difference at this size is that there is usually a clear process owner. Your outreach needs to reach that person, not just a senior executive.

**Example:** A 500-person healthcare company in Dallas with a VP of Workplace Strategy. She runs the site selection process, presents options to the CFO, and manages the broker relationship. The CFO will never respond to your cold email, but she will — especially if you demonstrate relevant market expertise.

### Companies with 1,000+ Employees

At enterprise scale, real estate is a dedicated function. These companies have Corporate Real Estate (CRE) teams, sometimes with dozens of people. They may also outsource portfolio management to a firm like CBRE, JLL, or Cushman & Wakefield through a master services agreement.

Before you prospect a company this size, you need to know whether they have an existing brokerage relationship for tenant rep. If they do, your outreach strategy changes entirely — you are either competing for a specific assignment or positioning for when the contract is up for renewal.

**Example:** A Fortune 500 retailer with a 15-person corporate real estate team. They have a global account with JLL but handle regional offices under 20,000 SF through a local broker panel. There is an opening, but only if you know it exists.

## How to Identify the Right Person

Knowing the general title is step one. Finding the specific person at a specific company is step two. Here are the most reliable methods.

**LinkedIn is your primary research tool.** Search the company page for titles like "real estate," "facilities," "workplace," or "operations." Go beyond the title — look at who is posting about office space, hybrid work, or workplace design. That person is thinking about real estate, which means they are either the decision-maker or closely connected to one.

**Check the company website.** The leadership or "about us" page often lists key executives. For smaller companies, this is the fastest way to confirm the CEO or COO's name and contact details.

**SEC filings reveal more than you think.** For public companies, proxy statements list key executives and their responsibilities. Annual reports sometimes discuss real estate strategy, expansion plans, or lease obligations. This is gold for your outreach because it gives you language the company uses internally.

**Call the front desk.** This sounds old-school because it is. But picking up the phone and asking "Who handles your office lease decisions?" works more often than you would expect. Receptionists and office managers often know exactly who that person is and will share the name without hesitation.

**Check public records for previous broker relationships.** Lease filings, property records, and even press releases about office moves can reveal who was involved in past transactions. If a VP of Operations signed the last lease, they are likely your target for the next one.

## What If You Cannot Find the Decision-Maker?

Sometimes, despite your best research, you cannot pinpoint the exact person. Here are three strategies that work.

### The Land and Expand Approach

Email someone adjacent to the decision — an office manager, an HR director, an executive assistant — and be direct about what you need. Something like: "I help companies like yours save on office leases. Could you point me toward the right person on your team to discuss this with?" People are surprisingly willing to make an introduction when you are polite and specific about what you are asking.

### The Multi-Thread Approach

Email two or three people at the company simultaneously. Send the CFO a message about lease economics. Send the VP of Operations a message about market conditions. Send the Head of People a message about workplace strategy. One of them will bite, and when they respond, you have identified your entry point. Just make sure your emails are different enough that it does not look like a mass blast if they compare notes.

### The LinkedIn Warm-Up Approach

Connect with the CEO on LinkedIn first. Most CEOs accept connection requests from local business professionals. Then, when you email the CFO, you can reference the connection: "I'm connected with [CEO name] on LinkedIn and wanted to reach out about your office lease, which I believe is coming up for renewal." This is not name-dropping — it is establishing that you run in the same circles.

## Tailoring Your Message by Role

Finding the right person is only half the battle. The other half is speaking their language. The same company, same deal, same market — but the email should read completely differently depending on who receives it.

**When emailing a CEO**, focus on strategic alignment. Talk about how the right space supports their growth plan, attracts talent, and positions the company in the market. CEOs do not care about per-square-foot comparisons. They care about whether the office reflects where the company is going.

**When emailing a CFO**, lead with numbers. Market leverage, potential cost savings, lease restructuring opportunities, and how current rates compare to what they are paying. CFOs want to know that engaging a broker will save or optimize spend. Give them a reason to believe that within the first two sentences.

**When emailing a VP of Real Estate or Facilities**, demonstrate market knowledge and process efficiency. These professionals evaluate brokers for a living. They want to know your track record in their submarket, your transaction volume, and how you will make their job easier. Skip the high-level strategy and show that you know the details.

**When emailing HR or People leaders**, connect real estate to employee experience. Talk about commute times, neighborhood amenities, hybrid work infrastructure, and how the right space helps with retention and recruiting. This audience sees the office as a talent tool, and your message should reflect that.

## Common Mistakes That Waste Your Outreach

Even experienced brokers fall into these traps. Avoid them and your hit rate will climb immediately.

**Emailing info@company.com.** This is the equivalent of throwing your letter into a black hole. Generic email addresses are monitored by junior staff or not monitored at all. Never use them for prospecting.

**Going too low in the org chart.** An office coordinator might manage the day-to-day space, but they cannot authorize a broker engagement. If you start too low, you end up pitching the same deal twice — once to someone who cannot say yes, and again to the person who can.

**Not multi-threading.** Relying on a single contact at a company is fragile. People change jobs, go on leave, or simply do not check email. Always have at least two contacts identified at every target company.

**Assuming the same title means the same role.** A "VP of Operations" at a 50-person startup has very different responsibilities than a "VP of Operations" at a 500-person enterprise. Do not assume the title tells you everything. Look at the person's actual responsibilities and background.

**Skipping the research entirely.** Spraying emails at every C-suite address you can find is not prospecting. It is spam. Five well-researched, well-targeted emails will outperform fifty generic ones every single time.

## Putting It All Together

The brokers who consistently win new business are not the ones sending the most email. They are the ones sending the right email to the right person at the right time.
